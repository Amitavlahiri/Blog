Do New Ideas Still Exist?

By Amitav Lahiri

Aug 9, 2026·4 min read·Op-Ed

In 1976, a court ruled George Harrison had stolen a song without knowing it. He had written "My Sweet Lord" certain it was his, but the melody seemed to belong to a hit from years before, a melody he had stopped thinking about for a long time. He wasn't lying. He had simply lost track of where it came from, and the moment he lost track, it began to feel like his own. This happens to you too, in smaller ways, all the time. A line for an essay that you had read years ago or an outfit you saw while scrolling reels may now be your own creation, and even the idea for this essay may originate from some video I saw when I was 15, but it's impossible to know for sure.

There is a word for this: Cryptomnesia, a memory that comes back without its label, so that remembering wears the costume of inventing. The source fades away, and the thought arrives feeling brand new. Think of a line you love, one you're sure is yours. Somewhere behind it is an origin: a documentary you half-watched, a paragraph you read once on a train, or a caption you scrolled past and forgot. One of them planted it. The note saying so is gone. So what is the real source if you can't tell the difference between what you invented and what you absorbed and forgot?

This is not a glitch you would want repaired. Imagine the opposite: every idea arriving stapled to its origin. This line is from the documentary; this one is something my uncle said; this is from a book I flicked through. You could never feel that anything was yours, and you could never use any of it freely. Forgetting is what cuts an idea loose from where it came from so it can be recombined and felt as your own. Borges saw the far end of it: there may be no clean first source at all, only one long text everyone keeps adding to and forgetting they did. The forgetting isn't actually the flaw in authorship. It is the condition for it.

I think this concept is especially relevant today because of the emergence of LLMs like Claude and ChatGPT. They work by processing millions of pieces of information, and then when prompted with a question they provide some sort of word-idea vomit. The charge against AI is that it only recombines what it was trained on and passes the results off as new. If it is true that all new ideas are simply old ones regurgitated after their sources have been forgotten, then this criticism against AI is also applicable to humans. We absorb books, films, conversations, and images while forgetting most of where they came from, and eventually something comes back out.

Obviously, this doesn't make a person and an LLM one and the same. ChatGPT does not suddenly remember something its grandmother said ten years ago and realize why it matters now. It does not become embarrassed by an old opinion or obsessed with one sentence for three weeks. But maybe AI forces a change in the definition of originality. If originality just means producing a combination of words or ideas that have never appeared in exactly that form, then a machine can probably be original, despite how bland its "originality" may be. That feels wrong, suggesting that originality must mean something more than just newness.

Maybe what matters is not where an idea came from, but why a particular person may have pulled these particular pieces together. Two people can read the same books, watch the same films, and hear the same conversations and still make vastly different things from them. The sources may not be original; even the combination may not even be completely original. But the selection of what survives, what gets forgotten, and what resurfaces is certainly original.

Originality was never a possession or a pure idea no one else has touched. Instead, it is a feeling, and that feeling is created by loss.

I couldn't show you the family tree of every idea in this essay. It is a messy amalgamation of the ones I invented, the ones I borrowed, and the ones I borrowed so long ago that they now feel invented.

I think new ideas do still exist, but we should stop expecting that they just come from nowhere.
