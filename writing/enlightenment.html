If You Achieve Enlightenment, How Will You Know?

By Amitav Lahiri

Oct 10, 2026·9 min read·Philosophy

In the sixth century, a Chinese monk named Huike came to Bodhidharma - the founder of Zen Buddhism - with a simple request: His mind was not at peace, and he wished to have it pacified. Bodhidharma replied, “Bring me your mind, and I will pacify it.” Huike searched and searched - and returned with nothing. “I cannot find it!” Bodhidharma replied, “There, I have pacified it”.

Huike was unable to locate the very thing he wished to examine. But why? The question of “How will you know?” assumes that an enlightened self is one that will seek an answer to that question. This essay argues that enlightenment cannot be verified, neither externally nor from within, and that the very desire to confirm it betrays an attachment to the self that enlightenment requires one to dissolve. Just like Huike searching for his mind, the seeker and the sought are one and the same.

Before asking how one would know, we must first ask what enlightenment is. Enlightenment is not a static concept; its meaning changes depending on where you are and which religion you believe in. In the Buddhist context, it refers to nirvana: the cessation of craving and the dissolution of one's self (Annata). Similarly, in Advaita Vedanta, enlightenment is the process of moksha, or the realization that the individual self (Atma) is one with the universal consciousness (brahmaan). Immanuel Kant says, “Enlightenment is man's emergence from his self-imposed immaturity” (Kant 1), immaturity being the inability to use one’s understanding without guidance from another.

Despite profound metaphysical differences across the three definitions, these traditions converge on the idea of a metamorphosis in one’s relation to ignorance, suffering, and/or reality itself. That is the definition this essay will be using. But even if a stable definition is found, can others confirm it for us?

One possible answer to this question is social ratification. The Buddha himself did not initially proclaim to be enlightened; He sat under the Bodhi tree, and others saw his transformation. Communities, traditions, and teachers developed around what they observed in him. In this way, an enlightened person might become aware of their transformation through the recognition and confirmation of others - the way Buddha or Christ is confirmed not by self-declaration but by those around them.

However, does the judgment of others prove enlightenment? Psychologist John Welwood coined the term “spiritual bypassing,” describing it as the “widespread tendency to use spiritual ideas and practices to sidestep or avoid facing unresolved emotional issues, psychological wounds, and unfinished developmental tasks”(Welwood 1). Practitioners can perform the external cues of enlightenment - including equanimity, compassion, and stillness - without ever going through the underlying change. This highlights how others can only perceive surface-level acts and changes and not delve into the deeper elements of enlightenment. Therefore, social ratification is unreliable because it only tells us who looks like they’re enlightened, not who actually is.

There is also a socio-cultural factor that plays a role: Who gets to be enlightened? That answer is shaped by caste, gender, class, and historical contexts. A Dalit mystic and a Brahmin scholar may have identical inner transformation, but only one gets to be socially ratified. This makes external verification not only epistemically unreliable but politically compromised as well. Then perhaps we must look toward our own judgment.

The problem is that the introspective self is not one that is neutral. Self-deception can be indistinguishable from truth inside the mind. Someone in the grip of pride or mania would feel exactly as certain as someone genuinely transformed, and there is no internal signal that reliably distinguishes between the two states. A person who feels certain that they are an enlightened being may begin to view any criticism as evidence that others are merely unenlightened. This kind of knowledge cannot be disproved. William James identified this as the “Noetic quality” of mystical states - or the overwhelming sense of having learned something profound and certain. However, he noted that these states remain “inarticulate” and carry only a “curious sense of authority”, not truth (James 370). The case of enlightenment is especially acute because, unlike other mystical states, enlightenment is not transitory; on the contrary, it is usually described as an eternal, final state of being. Therefore, there are serious implications for self-deception that go far beyond a temporary feeling. If one deceived oneself into believing that a particular emotional high point constitutes enlightenment, then this belief will structure one’s entire future. True objective judgment of one’s own transformation is impossible when the mind is capable of deceiving itself.

This problem of self-verification is exacerbated through L.A Paul’s concept of transformative experience. Paul argues that the subject undergoing a transformative experience is placed in a state of “epistemic poverty” (Paul 10), which is one's inability to grasp crucial information about their future experience simply because they have not had it yet. Similarly, one cannot set the criteria for recognizing enlightenment before you are enlightened because the pre-enlightened self lacks the perspective that is essential to define those criteria correctly. Yet if enlightenment truly transforms consciousness, then the post-enlightened self may no longer relate to the old criteria at all. The individual shifts mid-verification, so the very subject attempting to confirm enlightenment is transformed by the process itself. A transformative experience, far from being something to covet, may leave you worse off than before, for not only do you let go of your old self, you also lose the ability to judge who you have become.

At this point, the problem is no longer merely psychological but conceptual as well. The failure of both external and internal verification suggests that enlightenment is not a state that can be reliably confirmed. The question itself is paradoxical.

This is made clear in Buddhist and Vedantic traditions, wherein enlightenment involves the dissolution of the egoic self - referring not to arrogance, but to the sense of oneself as a separate individual distinct from others and the world. To ask “How will I know that I am enlightened?” presupposes the kind of self that enlightenment claims to transcend: a self attached to certainty, comparison, and possession. The question begins to undermine itself as the desire to secure enlightenment as an identifiable achievement may itself indicate attachment to the unenlightened framework. Someone who insists on their enlightened transformation reveals through that very insistence that an ego still remains. This is touched upon in Zen traditions through the concept of Makyo - the phenomenon whereby practitioners experience vivid sensory feelings of breakthrough during meditation. However, Zen masters consistently warn that such feelings are not enlightenment but egoic traps. Zen teacher Hakuun Yasutani states directly: “Never be tempted into thinking that these phenomena are real or that the visions themselves have any meaning. To have a beautiful vision of Buddha does not mean you are any nearer becoming one yourself” (Kapleau 42-44). Makyo literally means ”the realm of demons”, perfectly clarifying its deceptive nature.

Beyond the psychological and conceptual failures of verification, there is another glaring issue: language itself cannot do the work we are asking of it, raising the question of whether language can meaningfully encapsulate something that is said to transcend the world that language operates in.

Taking in the philosophy of Wittgenstein, we see that language derives meaning from shared “language-games” (Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations §23)- systems of use dictated by communal rules and forms of life. Verification only functions within such frameworks. Yet enlightenment is often described as a state that goes beyond ordinary conceptual distinctions entirely. When language attempts to force such an experience through a framework of verification, it does not just merely fail to produce certainty but also ceases to function coherently. As Wittgenstein famously concludes, “Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.” (Wittgenstein, Tractatus 7.0).

However, from Wittgenstein's own philosophy comes the strongest objection to this claim. Some truths do not require verification because they function as the background conditions through which all other things are understood. These assumptions are referred to as “hinge propositions”. They are not test truths but the preconditions of all inquiry: “If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put” (Wittgenstein, On Certainty §§341, 343). One does not ordinarily verify the existence of the external world before acting within it; it serves as the foundation of all subsequent perception. After all, you do not wake up each morning and gather evidence that reality exists before stepping out of bed. In the same way, enlightenment may not be something that needs external determination, but rather a change in perspective.

This argument is compelling because it moves away from needing any external proof at all. You don’t “prove” you are conscious before you actually experience it, just like an enlightened person may simply know their transformation firsthand. This idea is perfectly encapsulated in René Descartes’ cogito ergo sum - “I think, therefore I am.” Descartes argues that one’s own conscious experience is the one thing that requires no external confirmation; it is self-evident simply by virtue of being lived. An enlightened person may know of their transformation in the same way. Just because they can’t properly explain that certainty to anyone else doesn’t make it less real, much like failing to describe pain doesn’t mean the person isn’t suffering.

But the theory falls through in the end. This is due to the fact that hinge propositions are formed by human experience. There may be differences of opinion between individuals, but they all share the same fundamental reality, which includes their grasp of language, the ability to see physical objects, and the recognition of other minds. However, enlightenment is commonly thought of as being an entirely new departure from what it means to be human.

Going back to the tale of Huike and Bodhidharma. Huike couldn’t bring Bodhidharma to his mind because the seeker and sought were the same. That is the very essence of what enlightenment is - the realization that the self trying to find certainty is what blocks it.

This doesn’t mean that enlightenment is unreal or meaningless. Instead, it means that enlightenment may be the type of experience that cannot be approached. External recognition is not possible because enlightenment can be “gamed” and internal certainty fails because the mind cannot trust itself. Even language fails because enlightenment is repeatedly described as something beyond the ordinary. Therefore, the answer to the question “how will you know?” is that you cannot - and more importantly, that the desire to know is the final roadblock. The question is only present in the minds of the unenlightened.
