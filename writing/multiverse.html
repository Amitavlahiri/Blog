What's Left of You in Infinity?

By Amitav Lahiri

Jul 27, 2026·4 min read·Op-Ed

If you have consumed any entertainment from the last 10 or so years, you have likely come across the multiverse theory. Simply, it is the idea that our universe is not alone; instead, it is accompanied by countless other universes (also known as parallel universes) that exist simultaneously, making up all our reality. If this interpretation of quantum mechanics is truly plausible, then somewhere in some reality there may be a version of me who is 7 feet tall. There is a version in which my name is spelled "Aamitav," not "Amitav." Another in which I was born in March instead of January, the 23rd, not the 22nd.

This portrayal of the theory is commonly seen in films and TV shows, where an alternate self may have a scar that the original does not, have purple hair, or work as a chef instead of a lawyer. A theory of infinite possibility, but does anything stay constant?

If every single facet of your existence is variable: the color of your eyes, the exact second you were born, the exact configuration of neurons that makes you flinch at loud noises, the precise genetic roll that determined your height, the spelling of your own name, then any specific trait you point to and call "essentially me" is just one outcome among trillions, no more privileged than the others. It's not that alternate-you has different features. It's that there may be no version of you that isn't equally arbitrary. Then what anchors YOU in reality?

Philosophers have been circling a similar question for a while: The Ship of Theseus. What happens if you replace every single plank in a ship one at a time? Is it still the same ship? Or a new one entirely. Derek Parfit pushed harder: he argued that there may be no deep fact of personal identity at all, no further truth beyond a chain of overlapping memories, intentions, and psychological continuity linking today's you to yesterday's. According to Parfit, "you" was never a fixed thing being carried through time. It was closer to a pattern, a relay race where what matters is the connections between stages, not some indivisible baton passed along. The multiverse just makes this harder to ignore, because it removes the one thing that made the pattern feel continuous in the first place: a single timeline. If there's no timeline, only branches, which branch's pattern gets to be the real one?

The variation doesn't stop at height or hair either. James Baldwin said it poignantly, in a 1970 documentary filmed in Paris:

"What you've got to remember is what you are looking at is also you. Everyone you're looking at is also you. You could be that person. You could be that monster, you could be that cop. And you have to decide, in yourself, not to be."

He wasn't talking about quantum branches, instead, he was talking about circumstance. He highlights how thin the boundaries are between who you became and who could have become, given a different childhood, a different uniform, a different room to sleep in. If circumstance can rearrange your height and your name across infinite versions of you, it can rearrange your convictions too. Somewhere in that same infinite set isn't just a taller you or a you with one more letter in your name. There's a you who made the worse call, who became the monster, who wore the badge and used it wrong.

So if nothing, not your face, not your name, not even the moral choices you're proudest of, holds constant across every possible version of the circumstance that made you, what's left to call a self at all?

The psychologist Dan McAdams describes identity as a "life story." Not a fact you discover but a narrative constantly being constructed, revised, and lived inside, stitching together a coherent self out of a past that was actually just a mess of accidents and reversals. You are not the same person you were 5 years ago. Nobody's life is coherent in the way their sense of self is. The coherence is manufactured, continuously, by the part of the mind whose job is to make you feel like one continuous person instead of a sequence of disconnected events happening to a body. That manufacturing doesn't change across branches. The content changes completely: different face, different name, different childhood, sometimes worse decisions at the worse moment. But the function is the same in every version. Something is still doing the work of turning what happened into a story it can live inside, and still doing the work of choosing, right now, not to be the monster.

This means there's no essential you hiding under the contingencies, waiting to be found once you strip away the name and the body and the choices. It was never a noun. But it's not nothing, either. It means the search for a fixed self was the wrong search from the start. The real constant was never a thing you had; it's a thing you keep doing, in every version, everywhere at once, in the only way any of it ever could happen: right now, in this branch, deciding.
