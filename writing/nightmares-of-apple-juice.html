Nightmares of Apple Juice

By Amitav Lahiri

Oct 11, 2026·5 min read·Op-Ed

Last night, I went somewhere that does not exist.

It's still a bit hazy; I don’t remember too much. There was a building, I think, although I couldn’t tell you what it looked like anymore. There were people there who I knew, or did I? At a certain point, I was running from something. At another, I was sitting at a table having a conversation about something that must have been important. There was a whole geography to the place, a history to the people inside it, I felt everyone had a shared understanding of where we were. Then I heard this loud noise: BEEP BEEP BEEEEEEP. What was that? Then As I looked for the sound, I saw my white ceiling, my favourite blanket covering me, and sunlight shining through the window beside. Another Dream.

For a few seconds, I still had it. I could almost see the room, remember the people, understand what had made me so afraid. But as I lay there trying to hold onto it, the details began slipping away, like trying to hold sand as it escaped through your fingers. First the faces, then the buildings, then the conversation. Within a few minutes, the only remnant of the dream was the feeling it left me with.

How can my brain create something so vast without any effort from me? A zombie apocalypse, a bouncy castle up in the sky, a world bending in on itself like in inception. Consciously, I don’t think I could imagine a world as paradoxical and unusual as the ones I imagine when my eyes are closed. While most dreams leave me wanting to stay in that world, sometimes it can be the opposite.

But Strangely, My worst dreams are often not the most imaginative ones.

I have had nightmares about things so mundane that, once awake, I struggle to explain why they frightened me at all. Something as absurd and nonsensical as a waterfall of carbonated apple juice could somehow leave me in such dread and feeling of impending doom, that I start having physical palpitations. It is ridiculous. But inside the dream, the apple juice doesn’t sound funny, something about it feels deeply wrong and suddenly after waking up, something I could feel so vividly, was now once again slipping through my fingers. But why was I scared of it?

This made me question something I had always assumed was obvious. When I have a nightmare, am I afraid because of what I am dreaming, or is what I am dreaming simply my byproduct or visualization of an already-existing feeling of fear. Does the apple juice create the fear, or does the fear create the apple juice?

Psychologists Ross Levin and Tore Nielsen proposed something that may hint at the latter. Their neurocognitive model of nightmares describes what they call “affect load”: the emotional pressures accumulated during waking life that are carried into sleep. Normal dreaming, they argue, may help the brain process and reorganise emotionally charged memories. Nightmares can emerge when this process becomes overwhelmed or disrupted. In a later study where participants recorded nightmares and bad dreams for 21 days, affect load predicted how often disturbed dreams occurred, while what the researched called “affect distress”, how intensely someone tends to react to negative motion, better predicted how distressing those dreams felt.

This might explain why dream logic can be so disjointed while dream emotion can be so powerful. If the point is not to produce a coherent story, then the waterfall of apple juice does not need to be objectively frightening. It only needs to become the object onto which the fear is projected. The story can be nonsense while the emotion underneath it is perfectly coherent.

Furthermore, there is evidence that waking life leaks into dreams in this way. Dream researcher Michael Schredl has found support for what is called the continuity hypothesis: elements of our waking lives, particularly experiences that carry emotional importance, tend to appear again in our dreams. Yet they rarely return faithfully. The brain mixes them with older memories, different people and impossible environments. An argument from yesterday might not reappear as the same argument. It could become trapped in a room. Anxiety over an exam might turn into falling.

Maybe that is why interpreting dreams literally always feels slightly unsatisfying. Freud famously argues that dreams disguised unconscious wishes and conflicts, separating their obvious manifest content from a deeper latent content underneath. Modern psychologists are much more skeptical of the idea that every bizarre image hides a specific symbolic meaning. A waterfall does not necessarily “represent” one fixed thing. Neither does losing your teeth, being naked in public, or falling from the sky.

Even if Freud was often wrong, his questions were nonetheless very interesting. Perhaps the value of a dream is not in decoding each object, but in asking why the mind combined these memories, these people and this emotion on that particular night. The dream might not contain a secret message, but it was still assembled by a particular brain, carrying a particular set of concerns to bed with it.

Dreams are not completely fictional, nor are they memories. They seem to exist somewhere in between: fragments of real experience rearranged according to an emotional logic we do not consciously control or understand.

I may never know why a waterfall of carbonated apple juice terrified me, and maybe there is no clear answer, but for a few hours , my brain created a world in which it made perfect sense to be afraid.
