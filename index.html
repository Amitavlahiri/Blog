Do New Ideas Still Exist?

By Amitav Lahiri

Aug 9, 2026·4 min read·Op-Ed

In 1976, a court ruled George Harrison had stolen a song without knowing it. He had written "My Sweet Lord" certain it was his, but the melody seemed to belong to a hit from years before, a melody he had stopped thinking about for a long time. He wasn't lying. He had simply lost track of where it came from, and the moment he lost track, it began to feel like his own. This happens to you too, in smaller ways, all the time. A line for an essay that you had read years ago or an outfit you saw while scrolling reels may now be your own creation, and even the idea for this essay may originate from some video I saw when I was 15, but it's impossible to know for sure.

There is a word for this: Cryptomnesia, a memory that comes back without its label, so that remembering wears the costume of inventing. The source fades away, and the thought arrives feeling brand new. Think of a line you love, one you're sure is yours. Somewhere behind it is an origin: a documentary you half-watched, a paragraph you read once on a train, or a caption you scrolled past and forgot. One of them planted it. The note saying so is gone. So what is the real source if you can't tell the difference between what you invented and what you absorbed and forgot?

This is not a glitch you would want repaired. Imagine the opposite: every idea arriving stapled to its origin. This line is from the documentary; this one is something my uncle said; this is from a book I flicked through. You could never feel that anything was yours, and you could never use any of it freely. Forgetting is what cuts an idea loose from where it came from so it can be recombined and felt as your own. Borges saw the far end of it: there may be no clean first source at all, only one long text everyone keeps adding to and forgetting they did. The forgetting isn't actually the flaw in authorship. It is the condition for it.

I think this concept is especially relevant today because of the emergence of LLMs like Claude and ChatGPT. They work by processing millions of pieces of information, and then when prompted with a question they provide some sort of word-idea vomit. The charge against AI is that it only recombines what it was trained on and passes the results off as new. If it is true that all new ideas are simply old ones regurgitated after their sources have been forgotten, then this criticism against AI is also applicable to humans. We absorb books, films, conversations, and images while forgetting most of where they came from, and eventually something comes back out.

Obviously, this doesn't make a person and an LLM one and the same. ChatGPT does not suddenly remember something its grandmother said ten years ago and realize why it matters now. It does not become embarrassed by an old opinion or obsessed with one sentence for three weeks. But maybe AI forces a change in the definition of originality. If originality just means producing a combination of words or ideas that have never appeared in exactly that form, then a machine can probably be original, despite how bland its "originality" may be. That feels wrong, suggesting that originality must mean something more than just newness.

Maybe what matters is not where an idea came from, but why a particular person may have pulled these particular pieces together. Two people can read the same books, watch the same films, and hear the same conversations and still make vastly different things from them. The sources may not be original; even the combination may not even be completely original. But the selection of what survives, what gets forgotten, and what resurfaces is certainly original.

Originality was never a possession or a pure idea no one else has touched. Instead, it is a feeling, and that feeling is created by loss.

I couldn't show you the family tree of every idea in this essay. It is a messy amalgamation of the ones I invented, the ones I borrowed, and the ones I borrowed so long ago that they now feel invented.

I think new ideas do still exist, but we should stop expecting that they just come from nowhere.

What's Left of You in Infinity?

By Amitav Lahiri

Jul 27, 2026·4 min read·Op-Ed

If you have consumed any entertainment from the last 10 or so years, you have likely come across the multiverse theory. Simply, it is the idea that our universe is not alone; instead, it is accompanied by countless other universes (also known as parallel universes) that exist simultaneously, making up all our reality. If this interpretation of quantum mechanics is truly plausible, then somewhere in some reality there may be a version of me who is 7 feet tall. There is a version in which my name is spelled "Aamitav," not "Amitav." Another in which I was born in March instead of January, the 23rd, not the 22nd.

This portrayal of the theory is commonly seen in films and TV shows, where an alternate self may have a scar that the original does not, have purple hair, or work as a chef instead of a lawyer. A theory of infinite possibility, but does anything stay constant?

If every single facet of your existence is variable: the color of your eyes, the exact second you were born, the exact configuration of neurons that makes you flinch at loud noises, the precise genetic roll that determined your height, the spelling of your own name, then any specific trait you point to and call "essentially me" is just one outcome among trillions, no more privileged than the others. It's not that alternate-you has different features. It's that there may be no version of you that isn't equally arbitrary. Then what anchors YOU in reality?

Philosophers have been circling a similar question for a while: The Ship of Theseus. What happens if you replace every single plank in a ship one at a time? Is it still the same ship? Or a new one entirely. Derek Parfit pushed harder: he argued that there may be no deep fact of personal identity at all, no further truth beyond a chain of overlapping memories, intentions, and psychological continuity linking today's you to yesterday's. According to Parfit, "you" was never a fixed thing being carried through time. It was closer to a pattern, a relay race where what matters is the connections between stages, not some indivisible baton passed along. The multiverse just makes this harder to ignore, because it removes the one thing that made the pattern feel continuous in the first place: a single timeline. If there's no timeline, only branches, which branch's pattern gets to be the real one?

The variation doesn't stop at height or hair either. James Baldwin said it poignantly, in a 1970 documentary filmed in Paris:

"What you've got to remember is what you are looking at is also you. Everyone you're looking at is also you. You could be that person. You could be that monster, you could be that cop. And you have to decide, in yourself, not to be."

He wasn't talking about quantum branches, instead, he was talking about circumstance. He highlights how thin the boundaries are between who you became and who could have become, given a different childhood, a different uniform, a different room to sleep in. If circumstance can rearrange your height and your name across infinite versions of you, it can rearrange your convictions too. Somewhere in that same infinite set isn't just a taller you or a you with one more letter in your name. There's a you who made the worse call, who became the monster, who wore the badge and used it wrong.

So if nothing, not your face, not your name, not even the moral choices you're proudest of, holds constant across every possible version of the circumstance that made you, what's left to call a self at all?

The psychologist Dan McAdams describes identity as a "life story." Not a fact you discover but a narrative constantly being constructed, revised, and lived inside, stitching together a coherent self out of a past that was actually just a mess of accidents and reversals. You are not the same person you were 5 years ago. Nobody's life is coherent in the way their sense of self is. The coherence is manufactured, continuously, by the part of the mind whose job is to make you feel like one continuous person instead of a sequence of disconnected events happening to a body. That manufacturing doesn't change across branches. The content changes completely: different face, different name, different childhood, sometimes worse decisions at the worse moment. But the function is the same in every version. Something is still doing the work of turning what happened into a story it can live inside, and still doing the work of choosing, right now, not to be the monster.

This means there's no essential you hiding under the contingencies, waiting to be found once you strip away the name and the body and the choices. It was never a noun. But it's not nothing, either. It means the search for a fixed self was the wrong search from the start. The real constant was never a thing you had; it's a thing you keep doing, in every version, everywhere at once, in the only way any of it ever could happen: right now, in this branch, deciding.

Next: The List

Screenplay · "Tab 7"

The List

By Amitav Lahiri

Jul 1, 2026·6 min read·Short Film

TAB 7 THE STORY · (IN DEVELOPMENT)

THE LIST

A melancholic short film about a simple grocery run that slowly reveals a teenager living in the aftermath of loss. A handwritten shopping list becomes a relic, a lingering connection to Eli's mother, in a supermarket that feels detached from reality, caught between everyday life and mourning.

CHARACTER

ELI, 16

Dishevelled, tense, running an ordinary errand that keeps turning strange. Carries a list that isn't quite his handwriting, and holds onto it like it's the last thing tethering him to someone he's lost.

2ND CHARACTER

THE WOMAN

A familiar face in the produce aisle, always inspecting fruit with the same unwavering attention. Softens for a moment when she recognizes Eli, and asks the one question he isn't ready for.

3RD CHARACTER

THE MAN

Scruffy, unhurried, strangely at home in the dairy aisle. Tastes milk like a sommelier and talks about staring into gaps until you learn something about yourself. Harmless. Unnerving anyway.

4TH CHARACTER

THE LITTLE KID

Six years old, holding a plastic dinosaur, seeing more than a child his age should. Knows about the list, and about her, in a way Eli can't explain and can't shake.

5TH CHARACTER

THE CASHIER

Polite, tired, scanning without looking up. Asks Eli the question he's been avoiding all night, twice, without meaning to, and answers it for him before he can.

THE SETTING

THE SUPERMARKET

Fluorescent, too bright, too symmetrical. A space caught between everyday life and mourning, where every face Eli passes seems to carry an unsettling awareness of the emptiness he's hiding inside.

FADE IN:

EXT. SUPERMARKET PARKING LOT - NIGHT1.

A nearly empty lot. Incandescent lights flicker in a slow, uneven rhythm. The air is cold enough that Eli's breath ghosts up in thin tendrils.

ELI (16, dishevelled, tense) stands at the edge of the lot, hands in his pockets, staring at the neon supermarket sign.

A cheap plastic cart rattles across the pavement in the wind before veering off. Eli steps aside. Eyes dull but observant.

A distant Muzak version of a pop song leaks faintly from the store's outdoor speakers, tinny, too slow, warped around the edges.

Eli pulls a folded shopping list from his pocket. He unfolds it carefully.

  • Cereal (healthy)
  • Oranges
  • Milk
  • Ice cream (the right one!)

He studies the handwriting. Something about it feels familiar.

Eli stares at it, tracing the edges and curves of each letter with his eyes. He tucks the list away preciously.

As he steps toward the entrance, the automatic doors fly open, too early. Eli flinches. He waits, unsure if he triggered it. The doors remain open, humming softly. Finally, he walks in.

INT. SUPERMARKET - PRODUCE AISLE - NIGHT2.

Bright fluorescent light washes out the color of everything. Eli pauses near the entrance, blinking.

He notices: carts nested in perfect rows, a crooked SALE banner, a faint synthetic-cleaner smell, customers moving a beat too slowly.

He grabs a small basket and tests its weight absently.

Soft spray mist hisses over the vast array of produce: melons, apples, oranges, bananas, pomegranates, each accompanied by a plastic sticker to "guarantee" freshness. The fruits are arranged too perfectly. An unnatural symmetry.

At the very edge of the display, one pomegranate sits ruptured: skin split open, bleeding dark red juice onto the shiny white floors, like a quiet, sticky wound interrupting the order.

A WOMAN (40s, composed and meticulous) stands in front of the apples, sorting them with intense, almost ritualistic focus. She picks one up and lifts it toward the light the way a jeweller inspects a diamond for imperfections, then sets it down.

Eli recognizes her, someone he's seen here before, always in this aisle, always inspecting fruit with the same unwavering attention.

Eli approaches cautiously, looking for oranges. He picks one up.

WOMAN

They hide the bruises at the bottom.

Eli slowly looks over.

ELI

(soft, unsure)

Sorry... were you talking to me?

WOMAN

That's where they put them. Below everything else. People don't like to see damage up front.

Eli nods politely.

ELI

Yeah... that's how they get you.

He checks under the oranges. Nothing unusual.

WOMAN

You ought to pick carefully...

For the first time, she turns her head toward him. Her expression softens, as though recognizing him after a long time.

WOMAN

Not with your mother today?

Eli stiffens, shrinking into himself.

ELI

(stuttering)

Uh... no. She's... not in town.

They exchange faint, polite smiles, mismatched, uneasy. Eli hurriedly reaches back and places two oranges in his basket.

CEREAL AISLE3.

Rows of cereal boxes beam with bright colors and oversized mascots.

Eli stands between the shelves, holding the shopping list. He reaches for FIBREWISE BRAN, hesitates, sets it back. Reaches for SUGAR BOMBS, hesitates, sets it back.

A pendulum. Bran. Sugar. Bran. Sugar.

Blank paralysis.

A TEEN BOY (17, impatient, casual) pushes his cart behind Eli.

TEEN BOY

Dude. Pick one.

Eli startles.

ELI

Sorry. I'm... yeah. Sorry.

The boy grabs SUGAR BOMBS without slowing.

TEEN BOY (O.S.)

It's literally just cereal.

Eli lets out a tiny huff that might be almost a laugh, or a wince. Hard to tell. He reaches again for the bran but accidentally knocks a box sideways. It leans forward, slightly crushed at the corner. Eli stares for a beat. He's out of it. Doesn't react. He knows he must take the bran, and that's what he does. Moving on.

MAIN AISLE4.

A cart rolls across an intersection in front of him with no visible owner. Eli waits for it to pass.

The store feels longer than it should. The walls feel taller than they should.

PA SYSTEM (V.O.)

(distorted)

Attention, shoppers: the store will be closing soon.

Eli looks up. Blinks once. Walks on.

DAIRY AISLE5.

Rows of refrigerators hum softly. Cold air seeps out from the vents. Milk cartons line the shelves neatly.

A MAN (30s, scruffy, cracked glasses, missing teeth) stands motionless, staring at the milk section.

Without turning:

MAN

You ever look through the gaps?

His voice has a faint squeal to it, pinched and breathy at the same time. Eli freezes mid-reach.

ELI

...What gaps?

The man slowly traces a finger between the cartons.

MAN

These little gaps between the gallons.

(leans in, eyes against the glass)

Sometimes you can see straight through to the back.

He squints, waiting for something inside the fridge to move.

Eli tries to be polite.

ELI

I... don't really...

MAN

Ahh, man. I could just stand here all day...

(beat)

couldn't you?

ELI

Yeah... only got time for milk today...

MAN

Oh, then why didn't ya say so?

The man glides his fingertips across carton tops like he's reading Braille. Like a professional milk sommelier. He picks one. Unscrews the cap. Sniffs. Takes a sip. The man swishes the milk in his mouth as if he is tasting fine wine.

MAN

Hm. Almost.

He returns it to the shelf and repeats the tasting routine, more slowly, eyes closed, feeling for something unseen.

MAN

(shaking his head)

No... no, no. Not that one.

He puts it back. Eli stands stunned but tries not to show it. The man taps one particular carton gently, tries it, and giggles.

MAN

Ahh, Perfecto!

He lifts it proudly for Eli, his mustache dripping with milk.

Eli recoils.

ELI

Uhh... I think I'm gonna pass on that one. That... brand isn't my thing.

MAN

Ya sure, man?

ELI

Yeah. Definitely.

The man gently returns the carton, then resumes staring into the gaps. Silence.

MAN

You know... you learn a lot by staring at something long enough. Mostly about yourself.

Eli, unsure of what to say, slips past him, grabs a carton from the opposite end, and moves on.

DESSERTS AISLE6.

Warm lighting. Shelves packed with cakes, pies, puddings.

A LITTLE KID (6, solemn, quiet and unnerving) stands in front of the ice-cream freezer holding a plastic dinosaur.

Eli approaches. The kid doesn't move. Eli reaches for a random tub.

LITTLE KID

(quietly, without turning)

Isn't that the wrong one?

Eli freezes.

ELI

Uh... what?

LITTLE KID

You're looking for the wrong one.

ELI

(confused)

I'm looking for ice cream.

The kid wipes a circle through the fogged freezer glass. Rows of tubs sit perfectly aligned inside. He points at one.

LITTLE KID

Wasn't that her favorite?

Eli freezes. Silence. The hum of the freezers drops out, then returns, louder.

ELI

Her?

A beat.

ELI

...Do I know you?

The child turns toward him. Blank. Serious.

LITTLE KID

She told you to buy the right one... didn't she?

Eli pulls back.

LITTLE KID

Did you not read the list?!

ELI

I... don't... know?

LITTLE KID

You're forgetting again, Eli!

ELI

Wait... who are y-

The kid taps the freezer door with the dinosaur, a soft plastic thud, then walks off, disappearing around a corner too fast to track.

Eli stands alone, shaken, holding the right ice cream. He slowly places it into his basket.

CHECKOUT COUNTER7.

The store feels emptier, tighter.

Eli places his items on the belt:

  • Oranges
  • Bran cereal
  • Milk
  • Her ice cream

The CASHIER (20s, polite, tired) scans without looking up.

A beat.

CASHIER

(not looking directly)

Did you find who you're looking for?

Eli's breath catches.

ELI

What??

The cashier glances up, confused by his reaction.

CASHIER

(smiling politely)

Did you find what you're looking for?

Eli swallows.

ELI

I...

(tries again)

I don't know.

She hands him the bag gently.

CASHIER

You will.

The register beeps. Transaction approved.

Eli takes the bag slowly, almost reverently.

ELI

(thin, cracking)

Thanks.

He walks toward the exit.

EXT. SUPERMARKET PARKING LOT - NIGHT8.

The automatic doors shut behind him. Cold air settles over the lot, sharper now, the silence almost tender.

Eli adjusts the grocery bag, hesitates, then slips a hand into his pocket and pulls out the grocery list.

He unfolds it slowly, checking the items one by one, not because he doubts the groceries, but because he needs to see the words again. To make sure they're still there.

He notices a tiny rip along the crease. He feels his heart drop.

Eli tries to smooth it out with his thumb, careful, almost delicate, as if fixing the paper might hold something else together: his last memory of her. The tear resists, but he keeps trying, eyes glossing over with something he won't quite let surface.

After a moment, he folds the list back along the worn lines and tucks it close to his chest, then into his pocket.

He exhales, a quiet, uneven breath, wipes his eyes, gathers the bag, and walks off into the night.

FADE OUT.

FILM REVIEW

Yi Yi

Jul 23, 2026 • 3 min read

Dir. Edward Yang  •  2000  •  Cast: Wu Nien-Jen, Elaine Jin, Jonathan Chang

Drama Taiwan
QUIET
EPIC

Edward Yang's Yi Yi opens with a wedding and ends with a funeral, and in between contains what feels like an entire human life, perhaps even several. It is a three-hour Taiwanese film about a middle-class family in Taipei navigating ordinary crises: a failing marriage, a first love, a grandmother who won't wake up, a man rediscovering something he lost decades ago. Nothing in it is extraordinary. That is precisely what makes this film so special. Yang is not interested in the dramatic exception; he is interested in the texture of days, in the way life accumulates quietly around people until they look up one morning and find themselves somewhere they never quite decided to go.

The family at the center is the Jians. NJ, the father, is a soft-spoken engineer at a struggling technology company, a man who drifts through his life with the careful politeness of someone who has learned not to want too much. His wife Min-Min, after her mother slips into a coma, suffers a quiet breakdown. As the film goes by, or as life does, rather, she finds herself having nothing to say to her mother. She slowly realizes she is simply repeating herself, looking for something new to say. Noticing this, she retreats to a mountain monastery to find something she cannot name. Their teenage daughter Ting-Ting falls into her first love quietly and sideways: she becomes close friends with her neighbour Lili, and finds herself slowly drawn to Lili's boyfriend, Fatty. It is a romance that arrives wrapped in the particular guilt of adolescence, where the heart seems almost deliberately to choose the most complicated possible direction. Yang never melodramatizes this; he simply watches it unfold, the way these things happen, gradually, guiltily, and without clean resolution. And there is Yang-Yang, the youngest, eight years old, who walks through the film with a disposable camera, photographing the back of people's heads. When asked why, near the end of the movie, he explains, with the unnerving clarity children sometimes possess, that people can only see what is in front of them. He wants to illuminate the part they're missing.

That image, a child photographing the unseen half of the world, is as precise a statement of a film's purpose as any director has ever offered.

Yi Yi is built around the idea that we are all moving through our lives with a blind spot the size of ourselves. We cannot see how we appear to the people we love. We cannot know what we look like from the outside, in the middle of our own stories. We narrate ourselves constantly and still get it wrong.

Yang shoots Taipei in a quietly observational, naturalistic, and deeply patient cinematography style, characterized by long takes, wide tableau frames, and extensive use of reflections. He shows us the world exactly the way it looks. Similar to Wong Kar-wai's films, this movie leaves me nostalgic for a place I have never been to, in a time when I wasn't even born yet.

There is a sequence midway through where NJ travels to Tokyo on a business trip and reconnects with Sherry, a woman he loved thirty years ago and gave up. What follows is not quite a successful affair. It is something more melancholic and more honest than that. It is two people sitting across from each other in restaurants and hotel lobbies, speaking carefully about what happened and what didn't, discovering that roads not taken have their own haunting quality.

Next: Paris, Texas

FILM REVIEW

Paris, Texas

Apr 20, 2026 • 5 min read

Dir. Wim Wenders  •  1984  •  Cast: Harry Dean Stanton, Nastassja Kinski, Dean Stockwell

Drama Road Movie
MUST
WATCH

This past weekend I got to see a screening of Wim Wenders' film Paris, Texas. It has been on my mind ever since Travis drove into the sunset.

There is a shot, early in the film, where the main character Travis Henderson, gaunt, sunburned, mute, walks across the searing Mojave Desert in a red baseball cap, as if he has simply decided to walk until the earth beneath his feet runs out. We don't really know where he came from or where he's going, and neither, it seems, does he. That image, spare and enormous, sets the terms of everything that follows: this is a film about people who have gotten so lost inside themselves that they've forgotten how to exist near anyone else.

The film is, on its surface, a road movie and family reunion story. Travis is found by his brother Walt, brought back into civilization, and slowly reintroduced to his young son Hunter, or "Untuh" as Walt's wife Anne may say. Against the backdrop of a thriving Los Angeles where Walt runs his billboard business, Travis engages in a gentle, awkward reconciliation with his son, who has been living with Walt and Anne since Travis disappeared four years ago. The reunion between Travis and Hunter is not warm in any conventional sense. It is like two humans encountering each other for the first time, each arriving with no prior knowledge that another person like this existed in the world. Slowly, they learn to be in the same room, to communicate, and what I felt most poignantly, to walk on the same side of the road together. As their paternal bond begins to thrive again, there seems to be a piece missing: Jane.

The gentle reconciliation eventually becomes something more urgent: a road trip south, a father and son who are still mostly strangers to one another, pointed toward Texas in search of Hunter's mother, who has been absent even longer than Travis has.

Wenders and cinematographer Robby Müller shoot the American Southwest with a kind of reverent alienation. The rich vistas of endless highways, standalone roadside motels, billboards, and heat shimmering off blacktop are rendered as something simultaneously beautiful and hostile. These are not postcards. The landscapes feel like manifestations of Travis' psychological state: all that open space, and nowhere to put yourself. Los Angeles is fluorescent and grid-like, pulsating with exuberance and commerce. The Texas roads are vast and indifferent, the sky too large, the horizon always receding just ahead of you. The film understands that America's open spaces can feel less like freedom and more like exposure.

Running underneath all of it is Ry Cooder's masterful, twangy score. A slide guitar that sounds like it is grieving, like it is reaching for something it cannot quite hold, smoke slipping through your fingers. It never overwhelms a scene nor tells you how to feel. It moves beneath everything like a slow current, surfacing in the quiet moments, in the long, wide shots of fading daylight. It represents what cannot be said: something irreplaceable that has been lost, and that loss has a specific, aching texture that no one has quite found the words for yet, nor has Travis.

Those words, when they finally come, arrive in the film's shattering final act. Travis tracks Jane down to a peep show parlor in Houston, and what follows is what I believe to be one of the greatest scenes ever filmed. The two of them are separated by a one-way mirror and connected purely by a telephone receiver, speaking first cautiously, then openly, and devastatingly. Travis cannot bring himself to look at her directly. He turns away from the glass and speaks to her reflection as he recounts their story. We watch as Jane slowly realizes who is beyond the mirror.

Sam Shepard's screenplay, which carries a plainspoken, wounded poetry throughout, reaches its peak here. Jane describes her love and her longing in language that is simple and unbearable: that she used to make up speeches to him, whole monologues composed in her head for a man she couldn't speak to in person. And then she turns to something even more painful: that she missed Hunter so much she couldn't even bear imagining him, because to imagine would be to come undone. It is one of the most precise things ever written about a particular kind of grief, the grief where you protect yourself from the thought of someone not by forgetting them or hating them, but by refusing to let them exist even in your own mind, because the love is too enormous and too dangerous to look at directly.

Harry Dean Stanton (Travis) carries the whole film on a performance built almost entirely out of silence, out of avoidance, out of small, hard-won gestures. Travis is not sympathetic in any easy way, and the film never moves to excuse him. But Stanton makes his damage legible, makes his love for his son feel real and earned, and makes the decision to reunite Hunter with Jane and then vanish once more feel not like nobility but like the only honest thing a man like him knows how to do. His grief stands in the way of him being able to truly accept his role as Hunter's father, and the only way to get to something buried this deep is for him to go to where he was conceived: Paris, Texas.

Please watch this movie.
