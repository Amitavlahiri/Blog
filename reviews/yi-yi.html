FILM REVIEW

Yi Yi

Jul 23, 2026 • 4 min read

Dir. Edward Yang  •  2000  •  Cast: Wu Nien-Jen, Elaine Jin, Jonathan Chang

Drama Taiwan
QUIET
EPIC

Edward Yang's Yi Yi opens with a wedding and ends with a funeral, and in between contains what feels like an entire human life, perhaps even several. It is a three-hour Taiwanese film about a middle-class family in Taipei navigating ordinary crises: a failing marriage, a first love, a grandmother who won't wake up, a man rediscovering something he lost decades ago. Nothing in it is extraordinary. That is precisely what makes this film so special. Yang is not interested in the dramatic exception; he is interested in the texture of days, in the way life accumulates quietly around people until they look up one morning and find themselves somewhere they never quite decided to go.

The family at the center is the Jians. NJ, the father, is a soft-spoken engineer at a struggling technology company, a man who drifts through his life with the careful politeness of someone who has learned not to want too much. His wife Min-Min, after her mother slips into a coma, suffers a quiet breakdown. As the film goes by, or as life does, rather, she finds herself having nothing to say to her mother. She slowly realizes she is simply repeating herself, looking for something new to say. Noticing this, she retreats to a mountain monastery to find something she cannot name. Their teenage daughter Ting-Ting falls into her first love quietly and sideways: she becomes close friends with her neighbour Lili, and finds herself slowly drawn to Lili's boyfriend, Fatty. It is a romance that arrives wrapped in the particular guilt of adolescence, where the heart seems almost deliberately to choose the most complicated possible direction. Yang never melodramatizes this; he simply watches it unfold, the way these things happen, gradually, guiltily, and without clean resolution. And there is Yang-Yang, the youngest, eight years old, who walks through the film with a disposable camera, photographing the back of people's heads. When asked why, near the end of the movie, he explains, with the unnerving clarity children sometimes possess, that people can only see what is in front of them. He wants to illuminate the part they're missing.

That image, a child photographing the unseen half of the world, is as precise a statement of a film's purpose as any director has ever offered.

Yi Yi is built around the idea that we are all moving through our lives with a blind spot the size of ourselves. We cannot see how we appear to the people we love. We cannot know what we look like from the outside, in the middle of our own stories. We narrate ourselves constantly and still get it wrong.

Yang shoots Taipei in a quietly observational, naturalistic, and deeply patient cinematography style, characterized by long takes, wide tableau frames, and extensive use of reflections. He shows us the world exactly the way it looks. Similar to Wong Kar-wai's films, this movie leaves me nostalgic for a place I have never been to, in a time when I wasn't even born yet.

There is a sequence midway through where NJ travels to Tokyo on a business trip and reconnects with Sherry, a woman he loved thirty years ago and gave up. What follows is not quite a successful affair. It is something more melancholic and more honest than that. It is two people sitting across from each other in restaurants and hotel lobbies, speaking carefully about what happened and what didn't, discovering that roads not taken have their own haunting quality.

What makes NJ's reunion with Sherry particularly painful is that neither of them seems entirely sure what they are looking for. Perhaps they want closure, perhaps reassurance that the decisions they made were the right ones. But after thirty years, what closure could possibly be enough? Yang seems to suggest that regret is not necessarily the consequence of making the wrong decision, but an unavoidable part of making any decision at all. Every life we choose comes at the expense of another we will never get to experience. NJ eventually returns to Taipei, and Sherry disappears from his life once again. Nothing has really changed, except perhaps his understanding of what he has lost.

The film ends with Yang-Yang reading a letter to his grandmother at her funeral. He tells her that although he is only eight, he sometimes feels old. It is a remarkably simple line, but one that somehow captures the weight of the entire film. I think what moved me most about Yi Yi is that it made me think about how little of our own lives we actually understand while living them. We spend so much time wondering whether things could have turned out differently that we sometimes forget to look at what is right in front of us. And perhaps that is why Yang-Yang takes his photographs. Not to explain the world, but to remind us that there is always something we have failed to see.
